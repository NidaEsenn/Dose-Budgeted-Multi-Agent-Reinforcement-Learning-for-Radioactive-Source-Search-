Three Decisions for the Advisor

Author Nida EsenDate 3 November 2026Companion to Progress report

The progress report raised three questions. For each one this brief explains the problem in plain terms, summarizes what the literature and our own data say, compares the options, and gives a recommendation together with the objections a reviewer is likely to raise.

Recommendations at a glance

  • Decision 1, detector error: keep a realistic error model, but justify it with measured instrument tolerances (±20–30%), split it into a calibration part and a scatter part, and report results over a range of error sizes, not just one.
  • Decision 2, budgets: don't lower the budget to 0.05 Gy. Narrow the source strength to 10–100 TBq, the range of the real incidents the project cites. In our data, every budget violation came from that range.
  • Decision 3, main method: make version B (the limit on the chance of going over) the main method, with the team's limit split across robots. This matches an established approach in the literature. Keep version A as a comparison, and add two variants reviewers will ask about.
Decision 1

Should the simulator include detector error, and how large?

The problem

With a perfect detector, the robots found strong sources from the doorway and never needed to approach them, so there was no trade-off between information and dose. Adding a 30% error that depends on location restored the trade-off: robots that went close succeeded 96% of the time, against 81% for robots that stayed near the walls.

The danger is that a reviewer says "you invented noise to create the effect you wanted to study." The defence has to be that the error size comes from real measurements.

What the literature says

EvidenceWhat it showsSource
Regulator calibration rule for survey metersA meter counts as correctly calibrated if it reads within ±20% of the true dose rate, and its energy dependence must stay within 30%.CNSC (Canada)

I found no paper that reports a single "model vs measurement" percentage for robotic source localization. The value has to be argued from the instrument tolerances and scatter measurements above. The 2026 arXiv paper could not be opened from the build environment, so its residuals are unchecked.

Reasoning

  • The size is defensible. Instrument tolerances of 20–30% and scatter of 12% or more make a combined error of 20–30% reasonable. Our 30% sits at the upper end, so 20% should be the main value and 30% a stress test.
  • Our error has the wrong shape in one respect. Real error has two parts that behave differently:
    • Calibration error: each robot's detector reads consistently a bit high or low, everywhere.
    • Scatter error: depends on location and grows with distance, because scattered radiation fills the room roughly evenly while direct radiation falls off quickly.
    Our current model uses one location-based error that is the same size everywhere. The physical version makes the case for going close even stronger, and it would be a better story for the paper.
  • The estimator currently knows the exact error size. In reality it would have to be guessed. A test where the estimator assumes the wrong size would show the result doesn't depend on that inside knowledge.

Options

OptionGoodBad
A. No error (original requirements)Simple; follows the requirements exactlyNo trade-off, so the research questions become meaningless
B. Current: one location error, 30%Already built and tested; creates the trade-offA single chosen value is easy to attack; the shape isn't physical
C. Calibration + distance-dependent scatter, with a sweep over 0–30%Every number traces to a source; the sweep shows the trade-off appears as soon as error is realisticAbout a day of extra work; a few more experiment runs
Recommendation: option C. Ask the advisor:
  1. Is a combined detector and scatter error of about 20% (with 30% as a stress test) acceptable, given the CNSC ±20% rule and the scatter measurements?
  2. Should the error-size sweep be a figure in the paper? It answers the "you invented the noise" objection directly.
Decision 2

How do we make the budgets actually matter?

The problem

Acceptance check AC-3 says a team without a safety rule must go over budget in at least 30% of missions, so the safety method has something to fix. Our team without a safety rule went over in only 9% of missions (95% range 6–14%) at a 0.1 Gy budget. The earlier suggestion was to lower the budget to 0.05 Gy.

What our data says: it depends on source strength

I split the 200 test missions of the trained MAPPO team by source strength:

Source strengthMissionsSome robot over budgetFound the source
1–3 TBq520%83%
3–10 TBq510%92%
10–30 TBq555% (2–15%)82%
30–100 TBq4236% (23–51%)83%

Ranges in brackets are 95% confidence intervals. Each group is small, so these are indications rather than final numbers.

Every violation came from sources of 10 TBq or more. Half of all missions use sources weaker than 10 TBq, and in those the budget never matters. They dilute the average. For the strongest sources, the team already goes over budget 36% of the time, so AC-3 is met there.

What the literature and the requirements say

  • The real incidents cited in the requirements are all strong sources: Goiânia 50.9 TBq, Mexico 111 TBq, Samut Prakan 15.7 TBq. None are below 10 TBq, so 1–10 TBq doesn't represent the motivating cases.
  • A 0.05 Gy budget is hard to justify. Fukushima robot cameras and lidar failed at about 120 Gy (West et al. 2022), and a small single-board computer tested for robots failed at 111 ± 5.5 Gy (Nuclear Eng. Tech. 2022). That makes 0.05 Gy per mission 0.04% of a lifetime, about 2,400 missions. The requirements' own range, 0.2–2 Gy, is far easier to defend.
  • Safe-RL benchmarks don't use a universal threshold. The usual Safety-Gymnasium limit (a cost of 25) was picked for that benchmark, other papers set limits task by task, and published comparisons note that which method "wins" depends strongly on the chosen limit (Safety-Gymnasium; OmniSafe). The 30% in AC-3 is a sensible sanity check, not a standard. What matters is that the limit binds: an unsafe team breaks it clearly, and a safe team can still succeed.

Recommendation: B plus D. Use 10–100 TBq (keep 1–100 TBq for the generalization tests) and keep budgets inside 0.1–0.3 Gy. Reword AC-3 to: "a team without a safety rule exceeds the 5% target by a clear margin (at least 30% of missions), and a dose-aware baseline still succeeds in at least 70%". Ask the advisor:
  1. Can the main setting use 10–100 TBq, justified by the cited incidents, instead of 1–100 TBq?
  2. Is the reworded AC-3 acceptable?

Before the meeting I can run a short check: train MAPPO on 10–100 TBq at 0.1 and 0.2 Gy and confirm the violation rate. It takes a few hours of compute.

Decision 3

Which safety formulation should be the main method?

The problem

Version A limits the average dose, and it stayed switched off because the many easy missions hide the rare dangerous ones (Decision 2 shows exactly where those are). Version B limits the chance of going over, which is what research question 1 asks. But it was applied per robot, while the goal is for the whole team. The fix gives each robot 5% ÷ 4 = 1.25%.

What the literature says

  • Our fix is a recognised method. Chow et al. (JMLR 2018), a standard reference for chance limits in RL, handle a team-wide ("joint") chance limit by limiting the sum of the individual chances. That is exactly what splitting 5% across 4 robots does. They call it conservative: safe, but possibly stricter than necessary.
  • How strict depends on overlap. Ono and Williams (CDC 2008) point out that an equal split is extra conservative, and propose "iterative risk allocation": give more of the allowance to the parts that need it most. That is relevant to research question 3, where robots have different budgets.
  • Rare yes/no costs are noisy to learn from. A cost that is either 0 or 1, and is 1 in only a few percent of missions, gives a weak, noisy training signal (2026 paper). This is why the requirements allow adding a small per-step dose cost to version B, and why λ needed a larger step size.
  • Two alternatives reviewers will know about:
    • Saute RL (ICML 2022) aims for budgets that are never exceeded, by adding the remaining budget to what the agent observes. Our robots already observe their remaining budget, so this is closely related and should at least be discussed.
    • CVaR limits, which limit the average of the worst missions (CVaR-CPO), sit between versions A and B.

Recommendation: version B with the equal split and a small dose cost as the main method. Keep version A in the paper to show the "rare dangerous missions" effect; that is a result in its own right. Add the single team-wide limit as an ablation (it tests whether per-robot λ helps), and at least a discussion of Saute RL. Ask the advisor:
  1. Is B with the equal split the right main method?
  2. Should Saute RL be a full baseline, or only discussed?
  3. Is adaptive risk allocation worth planning for semester 2?
Limits of this brief

What isn't settled yet

  • The data comes from one training run per method and 200 test missions. The groups by source strength have only 42–55 missions each.
  • No paper was found that states a single "model vs measurement" error for robot localization. The 20–30% value is argued from instrument tolerances and scatter measurements.
  • arXiv and the publisher MDPI could not be opened from the build environment. Some paper details (Wright et al.'s formula, the 2026 robotics paper's results) come from search summaries and should be read in full.
  • The changes recommended here (new error model, 10–100 TBq, team split) still need short training runs to confirm.
