Background

The problem in one paragraph

A team of robots has to find a lost, very strong radioactive source in a room. Each robot carries a detector that counts radiation; higher counts mean the source is closer. Each robot also has a dose budget: the total radiation it can absorb before its electronics may fail. Going close to the source gives the best information but uses up the budget fastest. The research asks whether robots can learn to balance information against dose, and whether the team learns to give the risky close-up work to the robots that can afford it.

Terms used in this report

Glossary

Reinforcement learning (RL)
The robots learn by trial and error. They earn points (the reward) for good outcomes, here for making their guess of the source position more accurate. Over thousands of simulated missions they learn which actions earn points.
MAPPO
A standard learning method for teams. On its own it only chases the reward and ignores dose. We call this the unconstrained version.
MAPPO-Lagrangian (MAPPO-L)
MAPPO with a safety rule added. Each robot has a "price" on radiation, called λ (lambda). If a robot keeps going over its limit, its price rises and risky moves look less attractive. If it stays safe, the price falls back toward zero. The price adjusts itself during training.
Constraint version A
The safety limit is on the average dose: "on average, use at most 75% of your budget".
Constraint version B
The safety limit is on the chance of going over: "exceed your budget in at most 5% of missions".
Particle filter
How the team estimates where the source is. It keeps thousands of guesses (particles), each a possible position and strength. After every reading, guesses that fit the readings are kept and the rest are dropped, so the cloud of guesses shrinks onto the real source.
Baselines
Simple hand-written strategies used for comparison: random walking, perimeter (walk along the walls), approach (walk straight to the current best guess), greedy (move wherever the next reading is most informative) and shielded greedy (greedy, plus a rule that blocks moves predicted to exceed the budget).
Success
The team's final guess is within 1 m of the true source.
Violation rate
The share of missions in which at least one robot went over its budget.
Seed
A number that fixes the randomness of a run. One seed can give a lucky result, so the plan uses 10 seeds per method.
Gy (gray)
The unit of absorbed radiation dose. The budgets in this project are fractions of a gray per mission.
Findings

Four things the early checks revealed

1. As written, the problem was too easy

Fixed, needs approval
What we saw

With the physics model in the requirements, robots located the source from the doorway in 6–9 steps. They never had to approach it, absorbed almost no dose, and no robot ever went over budget, whatever budget was tried.

Why

The model assumes a perfect detector whose only error is the natural randomness of counting. These sources are so strong that the counts are huge, and that randomness shrinks to about 0.1%. Far-away readings were as good as close ones, so there was nothing to trade off.

Real detectors near strong sources are typically off by 10–30%, because radiation scatters off walls and equipment and calibration is imperfect. The simple formula leaves this out.

What I did

I added realistic detector error and tested two ways of doing it with 48 missions each:

Error modelRobots stay far (perimeter)Robots go close (approach)Does going close pay off?
None (original model)62%65%No: both stop early, often wrong
Fresh random error on every reading92%90%No: averaging many far readings removes it
Fixed error map for the room, 30%81%96%Yes

Numbers are success rates.

In the "fixed error map" model the error depends on where the robot stands, as wall reflections really do. Measuring again from the same spot gives the same wrong answer, so the only way to get a better reading is to move closer. This brings back the trade-off the research is about, so it is now the default.

Needs your input: this goes beyond the written requirements. It needs your approval and a published estimate of real detector error to cite. The original model can be switched back on with one setting.

2. Robots don't go over budget often enough yet

Open
What we saw

The plan requires that a team without any safety rule goes over budget in at least 30% of missions (acceptance check AC-3). Otherwise a safety method has nothing to fix. At a 0.1 Gy budget, unconstrained MAPPO went over in only 9% of missions.

Why

MAPPO learned to be efficient. It finds the source quickly and the mission ends, so it doesn't stay near the source long enough to collect much dose.

Needs your input: lower the budget to about 0.05 Gy, or reconsider whether 30% is the right bar.

3. Constraint version A can't control the real risk

Recommendation
What we saw

With version A the safety rule never switched on: λ stayed at zero, yet some missions still went over budget.

Why

Source strength varies a lot between missions. Most missions have a weak source, where robots absorb almost nothing; a few have a very strong one, where budgets can be exceeded. Version A limits the average dose. The many easy missions pull the average down to about 10% of the budget, far under the 75% limit, so the rule thinks everything is fine while the rare dangerous missions still go wrong.

It is like a speed limit applied only to your average speed over a whole year: you could still drive dangerously fast on a few days.

Recommendation

Use version B, the limit on the chance of going over, as the main method. It matches the research question directly. Version A stays in the study as a comparison, which is itself a useful result for the paper.

4. Version B's limit was per robot, but the goal is for the team

Fixed, not yet tested
What we saw

Research question 1 asks that no robot goes over budget in at least 95% of missions. That is a goal for the whole team, but the 5% limit was applied to each robot separately. In the test, each robot went over about 2.5% of the time, safely under its own limit, so the safety rule stayed quiet. Yet in 7–10% of missions some robot went over.

Why

With 4 robots each allowed a 5% chance, the chance that at least one of them goes over can reach about 20%.

What I did

Each robot now gets an equal share of the team's 5%: 5% ÷ 4 = 1.25% each. The team's chance of a violation can never exceed the sum of the robots' individual chances, so this guarantees the team goal. I also made λ adjust faster for version B; with the old setting it would have taken hundreds of thousands of steps to have any effect.

Next step: both changes still need a training run to confirm they work.
First test results

How the methods compare so far

All methods were tested on the same 200 simulated missions, with a 0.1 Gy budget and a cobalt-60 source. The two learned methods were each trained once and only briefly, so their rows show that the system works; they are not research results.

MethodFound the sourceSome robot over budgetMission length (steps)
Random44%6.5%85
Perimeter (walk along walls)83%7.5%20
Approach (go straight to the guess)92%47%28
Greedy88%10%16
Shielded greedy81%2%16
MAPPO (learned, no safety rule)85%9%27
MAPPO-L version B (learned, with safety rule)76%7%29
  • Learning works. During training MAPPO went from 46% to 85% success, and missions got shorter (from about 80 to 25 steps).
  • It doesn't beat the wall-walking strategy yet. 85% against 83% is within the margin of error. This comparison is acceptance check AC-4, and it needs longer training and 10 seeds.
  • "Approach" shows the trade-off clearly. It finds the source most often, but some robot goes over budget in almost half the missions.
  • Shielded greedy is the method to beat on safety, with only 2% violations.
