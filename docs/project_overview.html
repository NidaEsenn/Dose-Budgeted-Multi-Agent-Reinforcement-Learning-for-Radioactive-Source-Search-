Project overview · November 2026

Dose-Budgeted Multi-Robot Source Search

Author Nida EsenStage Prototype built, first checks done

A team of robots must find a lost, strong radioactive source while every robot stays within its own radiation dose budget. The most useful measurements are close to the source, which is also where a robot absorbs dose fastest. This project studies whether a robot team can learn to make that trade-off and share the risk.

In one minute

  • Problem: active search where information and danger sit in the same place, with a separate safety limit for each robot.
  • Approach: a simulator, a shared belief over the source position, and multi-agent reinforcement learning with one safety constraint per robot (MAPPO-Lagrangian).
  • Done: simulator, estimator, five hand-designed comparison strategies, the learning methods, the evaluation pipeline, and first short training runs. 55 automated tests pass.
  • Learned so far: the learned team finds the source and sends a single "scout" to the source on its own. But with strong sources the scout often uses up its budget and is stranded next to the source. The reason is in how the task is scored, and it can be fixed.
  • Next: fix the task definition, retrain with the corrected safety constraint, then run the full experiments on the computing cluster.
1 · The problem

Why it matters

When a strong radioactive source is lost or an accident happens, robots are sent in so that people don't have to go. But robots fail from radiation too. Cameras and lidar on the Fukushima robots failed after roughly 120 Gy, and a TEPCO cleaning robot had to be withdrawn after about two hours. Real incidents involve very strong sources, such as the 111 TBq cobalt-60 source stolen in Mexico in 2013, so a robot near the source can exhaust its safe limit in seconds to minutes.

The trade-off

A detector reads more counts the closer it is to the source, so close measurements pin the source down best. But the dose a robot absorbs grows like 1/distance². One metre from a strong source can cost a robot its whole mission budget in about 20 seconds; three metres away gives it several minutes. The team has to decide how close to go, for how long, and which robot takes the risky measurements.

In machine-learning terms

A cooperative, partially observable multi-agent problem with a cumulative cost constraint per agent (a constrained Dec-POMDP). Each step, every robot moves one cell or stays, absorbs dose, and takes a noisy count. A shared particle filter turns all counts into a belief over the source's position and strength. The team is rewarded for reducing its localization error. Each robot must keep its own cumulative dose under its budget, or it is retired on the spot.

What the project asks

These are sharpened versions of the questions in the requirements document. The changes add a pre-set success margin (RQ1), test robustness instead of a single trade-off curve (RQ2), and separate budget-awareness from robot identity (RQ3).

4 · How we plan to solve it

The pipeline

Simulator20 × 20 m room with obstacles, 3–6 robots, one source. Standard physics: inverse-square law, attenuation through walls, Poisson counts, and realistic detector error.
Shared estimatorA particle filter keeps thousands of guesses of the source's position and strength and updates them after every measurement.
Learning teamOne shared neural-network policy for all robots (MAPPO). A safety price λ per robot rises when that robot exceeds its limit (Lagrangian constraint).
Safety layer (optional)A shield that blocks moves predicted to exceed the budget, used as a backup and as a comparison.
EvaluationThe same 1,000 test missions for every method, 10 training seeds each, confidence intervals, and results split by source strength.

What we compare against

Five hand-designed strategies: random walk, walking the room's perimeter, walking straight to the current estimate, a greedy information seeker, and the same seeker with a safety shield. The learning methods are unconstrained MAPPO, MAPPO-Lagrangian with an average-dose limit (version A) or a limit on the chance of exceeding the budget (version B), and MAPPO with a fixed dose penalty.

6 · What we learned

Findings from the first checks

A perfect sensor model makes the problem trivial

With an idealized detector, strong sources produce such clean counts that the robots located the source from the doorway and never needed to approach. There was no trade-off left to study. Real detectors near strong sources are off by roughly 20–30% (calibration tolerances, scatter from walls), and this error depends on location. After adding it, approaching pays off: missions where a robot came within 2 m succeeded 91–96% of the time, against 60–70% when nobody did.

The learned team sends one "scout"

A simple "everyone approach" rule sends about three of the four robots to the source and exceeds budgets in 47% of missions. The learned team, with no instruction to do so, sends about one robot per mission while the others measure from a distance.

The scout gets stranded at strong sources

Almost every budget violation happens next to the source. For sources of 30–100 TBq, about 40% of approaching robots exceed their budget there and are retired. The learned team also approaches equally close whatever the source strength, so it isn't yet using its estimate of how dangerous the source is.

Why: the mission ends as soon as the team is confident about the source. A robot that strands itself on the final step costs the team almost nothing, and the extra-close reading even improves the score. The robot is playing the rules correctly; the rules don't yet say that stranding is bad.

Two problems with the safety constraint

  • An average-dose limit (version A) misses the danger. Most missions have weak sources, where dose is negligible; violations come from the rare strong ones. The average stays low, so the constraint never activates. A limit on the chance of exceeding the budget (version B) matches the research question.
  • The chance limit must be split across robots. A 5% limit per robot allows up to about 20% at team level with four robots. Giving each robot 5% ÷ 4 guarantees the team target; this is the standard conservative approach (Chow et al., JMLR 2018). It is fixed in code and awaiting a training run.

7 · Plan

Next steps

The desired behaviour in the danger zone: learn the source's strength from a distance first, pick a stand-off distance that suits it, measure from a few sides instead of standing on the source, keep enough budget to get out, leave once the estimate is good, and let the robot with the most budget take the closest reading. We don't hand-code this behaviour. We design the task so that this behaviour is what earns the most reward, then measure whether the robots learn it.

  1. Fix the task definition. Count a robot that can't get back out as a failure (keep a dose reserve for the exit), so stranding is no longer free.
  2. Give each robot a predicted-dose reading for its next possible moves, computed from the team's estimate. A real robot could compute this too.
  3. Retrain with the corrected safety constraint (version B, limit split across robots), and focus the main experiments on strong sources (10–100 TBq, the range of the real incidents).
  4. Add metrics for the goal: how often an approaching robot is stranded, how often it approaches and then leaves, and whether its stand-off distance grows with source strength.
  5. Run the full experiments on the cluster: 10 seeds per method, about 370 training runs.

9 · Limits

What these results are not

  • Every number here comes from one short training run per method and 200 test missions. They show the system works; they are not research results.
  • Everything is simulated. The detector-error model is central to the results and needs a stronger basis in the literature; the plan is to report results across a range of error sizes.
  • A few physical constants and the exact detector formula still need to be checked against primary sources.
